# Stocks and Ownership

## One-Line Summary

A stock share is an equity claim on a company: it can benefit from company success through price appreciation or dividends, but it can also lose value, including all invested capital.

## What a Share Represents

Owning stock means holding an equity security issued by a company. In practical terms, the owner participates economically in the company's success or failure rather than lending money with a fixed repayment schedule. FINRA frames stock ownership as part ownership in the issuing company, while Investor.gov emphasizes that public companies generally file ongoing reports with the SEC.

That ownership is indirect and bounded. A common shareholder does not own a specific factory, bank account, patent, or product line. The share is a claim whose market value depends on expected future cash flows, risk, interest rates, sentiment, liquidity, and the supply and demand for the shares.

## Why Stock Can Return Money

Stocks can create investor return in two main ways:

- Price appreciation: the market later pays more for the share than the investor paid.
- Dividends: the company distributes part of its earnings or cash to shareholders.

Neither route is guaranteed. A company can perform well while its stock performs poorly if expectations were already too high. A company can also suspend dividends, issue more shares, take on debt, lose competitiveness, or fail.

## Public Company Visibility

Public companies listed on national exchanges are subject to recurring disclosure requirements. For a learner, that means the first evidence layer is not social media commentary or a chart pattern. The first layer is company filings: 10-K annual reports, 10-Q quarterly reports, 8-K current reports, proxy statements, and other SEC disclosures available through EDGAR.

## Core Vocabulary

| Term | Working meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Share | Unit of equity ownership in a company |
| Ticker | Short exchange symbol used to identify a traded security |
| Market capitalization | Share price multiplied by shares outstanding |
| Dividend | Cash or stock distribution to shareholders |
| Earnings | Profit after expenses, taxes, and other costs under accounting rules |
| Float | Shares available for public trading |
| Liquidity | Ability to buy or sell without large price impact |

## Mental Model

Read a stock as three connected objects:

1. Business: what the company does, how it earns money, and what risks threaten it.
2. Security: the actual tradable share with voting, dividend, dilution, and listing features.
3. Market price: what buyers and sellers are willing to exchange it for right now.

Confusing those three objects is a common beginner error. A good business is not automatically a good stock at any price, and a rising stock is not automatically a good business.
