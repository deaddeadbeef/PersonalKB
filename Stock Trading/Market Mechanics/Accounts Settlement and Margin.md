# Accounts Settlement and Margin

## One-Line Summary

Account type controls what money you can trade with, when trades settle, whether you can borrow, and how quickly a mistake can become an account restriction.

## Cash Account

In a cash account, purchases must be paid for in full. FINRA explains that if you buy securities in a cash account, you must pay for them before selling them, and frequent trading requires attention to settlement cycles and available settled funds.

Cash accounts can still create problems. Buying and selling before funds settle can lead to cash trading violations. The account may feel simple, but settlement timing still matters.

## Settlement

Settlement is the official transfer of securities to the buyer and cash to the seller. Investor.gov states that the U.S. standard settlement cycle for applicable securities transactions changed to T+1 for transactions on or after May 28, 2024. Covered securities include stocks, bonds, municipal securities, ETFs, certain mutual funds, and limited partnerships that trade on an exchange.

As of 2026-07-01, use T+1 as the current U.S. baseline for most stock and ETF trades, but recheck before relying on it. Brokers can also impose operational rules that matter in practice.

## Margin Account

In a margin account, the brokerage firm can lend money against securities. FINRA describes margin accounts as the account type used for borrowing to buy securities and for short selling. Margin can increase buying power, but it also increases the possibility of larger losses, including losses beyond the original deposit.

FINRA's 2026 investor education describes new intraday margin requirements that replace older day-trading margin provisions in key guidance. The newer framing focuses on whether the account has adequate equity relative to intraday positions. FINRA states that leveraged trading on margin requires at least $2,000 in equity, while brokerage firms can impose higher house requirements.

## Short Selling

Short selling generally means selling a stock the investor does not own, expecting to buy it back later at a lower price. The risk is asymmetric: a long position can fall to zero, but a short position can keep losing as price rises. Investor.gov characterizes short selling as for experienced investors.

## First-Pass Rule

For this wiki's beginner phase:

- Use cash-account mechanics for understanding.
- Avoid margin.
- Avoid short selling.
- Avoid intraday frequent-trading strategies.
- Use paper-trading entries to practice order and settlement logic before any real account action.
