## Book I: Origins And Falcon

Begin with founding, Falcon 1 survival, Falcon 9 maturation, and fleet operations.

- [[SpaceX/Origins and History/Origins and History Overview|Origins and History Overview]] — SpaceX was founded in 2002 by Elon Musk with the audacious goal of reducing space transportation costs and enabling the colonization of Mars.
- [[SpaceX/Origins and History/Key Milestones Timeline|Key Milestones Timeline]] — SpaceX's timeline shows a pattern of building one capability, operationalizing it, and then using it to unlock the next one faster.
- [[SpaceX/Origins and History/Elon Musk's Mars Vision|Elon Musk's Mars Vision]] — Musk's Mars vision treats settlement on Mars as the best long-term hedge against civilization-ending risks on Earth.
- [[SpaceX/Origins and History/Failures Recovery and Lessons Learned|Failures Recovery and Lessons Learned]] — SpaceX uses failures as compressed learning cycles rather than treating them as reasons to stop the program.
- [[SpaceX/Origins and History/Falcon 1 Program|Falcon 1 Program]] — Falcon 1 was SpaceX's minimum viable orbital rocket and the company's first real proof that private launch development could work.
- [[SpaceX/Origins and History/Founding of SpaceX|Founding of SpaceX]] — SpaceX was founded because Musk concluded it would be cheaper and more transformative to build rockets than to buy them.
- [[SpaceX/Origins and History/SpaceX Culture and Operations|SpaceX Culture and Operations]] — SpaceX's culture is designed to shorten the distance between idea, hardware, test, failure, and redesign.
- [[SpaceX/Falcon Program/Falcon Program Overview|Falcon Program Overview]] — The Falcon family of launch vehicles — Falcon 9 and Falcon Heavy — transformed the commercial launch market by combining reliable performance with unprecedented cost efficiency.
- [[SpaceX/Falcon Program/Block 5 and Fleet Management|Block 5 and Fleet Management]] — Block 5 turned a rocket into a reusable fleet vehicle by designing every component — from bolted engines to titanium fins — for dozens of flights with minimal refurbishment.
- [[SpaceX/Falcon Program/Falcon 9 Architecture|Falcon 9 Architecture]] — Falcon 9 is a two-stage LOX/RP-1 rocket whose reusable first stage lands itself after every mission, fundamentally resetting launch economics.
- [[SpaceX/Falcon Program/Falcon 9 Evolution|Falcon 9 Evolution]] — Falcon 9 evolved through five rapid hardware iterations — each version folding in lessons from the last — more than doubling LEO capacity from ~10,450 kg to ~22,800 kg in eight years.
- [[SpaceX/Falcon Program/Falcon Heavy Design and Missions|Falcon Heavy Design and Missions]] — Falcon Heavy straps three Falcon 9 first stages together, tripling liftoff thrust to open super-heavy-lift missions at a fraction of historical costs.
- [[SpaceX/Falcon Program/Falcon Performance Specifications|Falcon Performance Specifications]] — Falcon 9 and Falcon Heavy share the same Merlin engine family and LOX/RP-1 propellants, scaling from 9 to 27 engines to cover everything from routine LEO missions to interplanetary probes.
- [[SpaceX/Falcon Program/Launch Cadence and Turnaround Records|Launch Cadence and Turnaround Records]] — SpaceX's launch cadence has grown exponentially — from 26 launches in 2020 to 134 in 2024 — driven by reusable boosters, captive Starlink demand, and streamlined pad operations.

## Book II: Engines, Reuse, And Human Spaceflight

Read the engineering systems that made cadence, crew transport, and cost change possible.

- [[SpaceX/Engines and Propulsion/Engines and Propulsion Overview|Engines and Propulsion Overview]] — Rocket engines are the most complex and performance-critical components in any launch vehicle, and SpaceX's engine development has been central to its competitive advantage.
- [[SpaceX/Engines and Propulsion/Draco and SuperDraco Thrusters|Draco and SuperDraco Thrusters]] — Draco is Dragon's everyday manoeuvring thruster, while SuperDraco is the much larger abort engine kept ready for emergencies.
- [[SpaceX/Engines and Propulsion/Engine Manufacturing and Testing|Engine Manufacturing and Testing]] — SpaceX treats manufacturing and testing as one rapid loop: build hardware, fire it, learn from it, and feed improvements back into production.
- [[SpaceX/Engines and Propulsion/Full-Flow Staged Combustion Cycle|Full-Flow Staged Combustion Cycle]] — A full-flow staged combustion engine uses two preburners so that both propellants are completely gasified before entering the main chamber.
- [[SpaceX/Engines and Propulsion/Merlin Engine Family|Merlin Engine Family]] — Merlin trades some peak thermodynamic efficiency for a simpler, cheaper, highly reusable engine architecture.
- [[SpaceX/Engines and Propulsion/Raptor Engine|Raptor Engine]] — Raptor combines a methane fuel choice with a full-flow staged combustion cycle to maximise efficiency, thrust, and reusability.
- [[SpaceX/Engines and Propulsion/Raptor Evolution and Raptor 3|Raptor Evolution and Raptor 3]] — Raptor evolution is mostly about simplification: fewer parts, less external hardware, higher thrust, and easier reuse.
- [[SpaceX/Reusability/Reusability Overview|Reusability Overview]] — Reusability is the single most important innovation SpaceX has brought to the launch industry.
- [[SpaceX/Reusability/Booster Recovery and Reflight|Booster Recovery and Reflight]] — Recovering a booster is only half the equation—reflying it quickly, safely, and affordably is what turns a landing into an economic revolution.
- [[SpaceX/Reusability/Fairing Recovery and Reuse|Fairing Recovery and Reuse]] — The payload fairing—a $6M carbon-fiber nose cone—was traditionally thrown away after every launch; SpaceX figured out how to fish it out of the ocean and fly it again.
- [[SpaceX/Reusability/Landing Hardware|Landing Hardware]] — Four hardware systems — grid fins, landing legs, cold gas thrusters, and drone ships — work together to steer, stabilize, and catch a returning booster.
- [[SpaceX/Reusability/Propulsive Landing Technology|Propulsive Landing Technology]] — SpaceX reignites the booster's engines three times during descent — boostback, entry, and landing — to guide a falling rocket stage to a precise vertical touchdown.
- [[SpaceX/Reusability/Reusability Economics|Reusability Economics]] — By reflying the same booster 20+ times, SpaceX amortizes the ~$30–35M build cost across many missions, driving per-launch cost toward the marginal floor of propellant + operations.
- [[SpaceX/Reusability/Starship Catch System|Starship Catch System]] — Instead of carrying landing legs on every flight, catch the returning Super Heavy booster with giant arms on the launch tower itself.
- [[SpaceX/Dragon and Human Spaceflight/Dragon and Human Spaceflight Overview|Dragon and Human Spaceflight Overview]] — Dragon is SpaceX's spacecraft family for cargo delivery and crew transport to the International Space Station and beyond.
- [[SpaceX/Dragon and Human Spaceflight/Cargo Dragon|Cargo Dragon]] — Cargo Dragon replaced much of the Space Shuttle's cargo-delivery role with a privately developed spacecraft that can both deliver supplies to the ISS and return important cargo safely to Earth.
- [[SpaceX/Dragon and Human Spaceflight/Commercial Crew Development|Commercial Crew Development]] — Commercial Crew used phased, fixed-price partnerships to help companies like SpaceX build and certify human-rated spacecraft for ISS transport.
- [[SpaceX/Dragon and Human Spaceflight/Crew Dragon Design|Crew Dragon Design]] — Crew Dragon is built as a modern orbital crew capsule that can launch astronauts, protect them during emergencies, dock itself to the ISS, and return them safely through reentry and splashdown.
- [[SpaceX/Dragon and Human Spaceflight/Life Support and Abort Systems|Life Support and Abort Systems]] — Crew Dragon's survival systems are designed so the crew can breathe, stay thermally stable, escape launch failures, endure reentry, and land safely in both normal and emergency cases.
- [[SpaceX/Dragon and Human Spaceflight/Operational Crew Missions|Operational Crew Missions]] — Once Crew Dragon entered service, NASA and its partners gained a repeatable launch-to-station-to-return cycle for rotating long-duration ISS crews.
- [[SpaceX/Dragon and Human Spaceflight/Private Astronaut Missions|Private Astronaut Missions]] — Private astronaut missions showed that Crew Dragon could support people and mission profiles outside the traditional government-astronaut model.

## Book III: Starship And Starlink

Pair the Mars-class vehicle with the satellite business intended to fund and use it.

- [[SpaceX/Starship Program/Starship Program Overview|Starship Program Overview]] — Starship is SpaceX's fully reusable super-heavy-lift launch system designed to carry both crew and cargo to Earth orbit, the Moon, Mars, and beyond.
- [[SpaceX/Starship Program/Human Landing System|Human Landing System]] — Starship HLS is a Moon-optimized version of Starship built to ferry astronauts between lunar orbit and the lunar surface as part of Artemis.
- [[SpaceX/Starship Program/Integrated Flight Tests|Integrated Flight Tests]] — The IFT campaign develops Starship by flying the full system early and often, then folding failures and successes directly into the next vehicle.
- [[SpaceX/Starship Program/Orbital Refueling|Orbital Refueling]] — Orbital refueling lets Starship refill after reaching low Earth orbit so it can still have enough propellant left for deep-space missions.
- [[SpaceX/Starship Program/Starship Variants and Applications|Starship Variants and Applications]] — Starship is a shared spacecraft platform whose common structure can be customized into different mission-specific variants. Analogy: Like a shipping container standard — one size frame, infinite cargo configurations.
- [[SpaceX/Starship Program/Starship Vehicle Architecture|Starship Vehicle Architecture]] — Starship is a two-stage launch system built so both major pieces can return and fly again, combining super-heavy lift with spacecraft-like reusability.
- [[SpaceX/Starship Program/Super Heavy Booster|Super Heavy Booster]] — Super Heavy is the reusable first stage that provides the immense thrust needed to launch the full Starship stack and then return for recovery.
- [[SpaceX/Starlink/Starlink Overview|Starlink Overview]] — Starlink is SpaceX's satellite internet constellation designed to provide high-speed, low-latency broadband connectivity worldwide.
- [[SpaceX/Starlink/Constellation Design and Orbits|Constellation Design and Orbits]] — Starlink achieves global broadband by spreading many satellites across different low-Earth orbit shells, each tuned for coverage, capacity, or latitude.
- [[SpaceX/Starlink/Direct-to-Cell Technology|Direct-to-Cell Technology]] — Starlink direct-to-cell turns certain satellites into orbiting LTE base stations that can talk to normal phones without special hardware.
- [[SpaceX/Starlink/Ground Infrastructure|Ground Infrastructure]] — Starlink only becomes usable internet when satellites hand traffic down to ground gateways and user terminals carry that link into homes, vehicles, ships, and aircraft.
- [[SpaceX/Starlink/Laser Inter-Satellite Links|Laser Inter-Satellite Links]] — Laser inter-satellite links turn Starlink from a set of isolated relay satellites into a connected orbital mesh network.
- [[SpaceX/Starlink/Satellite Generations|Satellite Generations]] — Each new Starlink satellite generation packs more networking capability into each launch, so the constellation gets better not just by adding satellites but by upgrading the spacecraft themselves.
- [[SpaceX/Starlink/Starlink Business Model|Starlink Business Model]] — Starlink makes money by selling recurring connectivity across multiple customer tiers, then uses that cash flow to expand the network and fund future SpaceX programs.

## Book IV: Facilities, Missions, And Deep Dives

Study the operations layer and the technical subsystems that make high cadence credible.

- [[SpaceX/Launch Operations and Facilities/Launch Operations and Facilities Overview|Launch Operations and Facilities Overview]] — SpaceX operates a network of launch sites, test facilities, and manufacturing centers that enable the highest launch cadence in the world.
- [[SpaceX/Launch Operations and Facilities/Cape Canaveral SLC-40|Cape Canaveral SLC-40]] — SLC-40 is the workhorse launch pad that enables SpaceX to fly the majority of its East Coast Falcon 9 missions at very high tempo.
- [[SpaceX/Launch Operations and Facilities/Kennedy Space Center LC-39A|Kennedy Space Center LC-39A]] — LC-39A is SpaceX's most historically significant pad because it bridges the Apollo era, the Space Shuttle era, and modern commercial crewed spaceflight.
- [[SpaceX/Launch Operations and Facilities/McGregor Test Facility|McGregor Test Facility]] — McGregor is the place where SpaceX verifies that propulsion hardware actually works before it is trusted on a mission.
- [[SpaceX/Launch Operations and Facilities/Mission Control and Launch Operations|Mission Control and Launch Operations]] — Mission control coordinates the rocket, ground systems, weather, range, and payload so a Falcon 9 can move from fueling to orbit on a precise timeline.
- [[SpaceX/Launch Operations and Facilities/Starbase Boca Chica|Starbase Boca Chica]] — Starbase is not just a launch pad; it is a combined factory, test site, and operational base for Starship and Super Heavy.
- [[SpaceX/Launch Operations and Facilities/Vandenberg SLC-4E|Vandenberg SLC-4E]] — SLC-4E exists because some orbits that are unsafe or inefficient from Florida become practical from California's Pacific coast.
- [[SpaceX/Missions and Payloads/Missions and Payloads Overview|Missions and Payloads Overview]] — SpaceX's mission manifest is the empirical record of everything the company has achieved — from the first Falcon 1 flight to over 300 Falcon 9 launches and beyond.
- [[SpaceX/Missions and Payloads/Demonstration and Test Flights|Demonstration and Test Flights]] — SpaceX uses demonstration flights to prove new capabilities before turning them into operational services and contracts.
- [[SpaceX/Missions and Payloads/Mission Success Rate and Statistics|Mission Success Rate and Statistics]] — SpaceX's launch record shows that high reliability and rapid reuse can coexist at unprecedented scale. Analogy: Like a batting average that keeps climbing — 300+ consecutive successes is an unmatched reliability record.
- [[SpaceX/Missions and Payloads/NASA Science and Cargo Missions|NASA Science and Cargo Missions]] — SpaceX turned a cargo-services partnership with NASA into a broader role launching some of the agency's most important science missions.
- [[SpaceX/Missions and Payloads/National Security Launches|National Security Launches]] — SpaceX had to prove it could meet the strictest mission-assurance standards before it could win sensitive U.S. defense and intelligence launches.
- [[SpaceX/Missions and Payloads/Notable Commercial Launches|Notable Commercial Launches]] — SpaceX won commercial launch customers by pairing lower prices with proven mission performance, then used paying missions to normalize booster reuse.
- [[SpaceX/Missions and Payloads/Rideshare and Transporter Missions|Rideshare and Transporter Missions]] — SpaceX used Falcon 9's excess capacity to turn orbital launch into a shared service for small satellites. Analogy: Like a bus route to orbit — dozens of small passengers sharing one big ride at a fraction of the cost.
- [[SpaceX/Technology Deep Dives/Technology Deep Dives Overview|Technology Deep Dives Overview]] — SpaceX's competitive edge rests on a series of engineering innovations that span avionics, materials, manufacturing, and flight systems.
- [[SpaceX/Technology Deep Dives/Autogenous Pressurization|Autogenous Pressurization]] — Starship keeps tank pressure up by feeding back hot gaseous versions of its own propellants instead of carrying a separate helium pressurization system.
- [[SpaceX/Technology Deep Dives/Autonomous Flight Termination System|Autonomous Flight Termination System]] — AFTS replaces a ground officer's destruct command with an onboard computer that monitors the rocket's trajectory and acts automatically if it becomes unsafe.
- [[SpaceX/Technology Deep Dives/Avionics and Flight Software|Avionics and Flight Software]] — SpaceX uses multiple commercial flight computers running the same software and trusts the majority answer, gaining modern computing performance while tolerating single-computer faults.
- [[SpaceX/Technology Deep Dives/Grid Fins and Aerodynamic Control|Grid Fins and Aerodynamic Control]] — Falcon 9 uses four deployable grid fins to steer the booster through the atmosphere after stage separation until the landing burn takes over.
- [[SpaceX/Technology Deep Dives/Manufacturing Innovation|Manufacturing Innovation]] — SpaceX treats rocket manufacturing more like an integrated high-speed factory system than a slow outsourced aerospace program.
- [[SpaceX/Technology Deep Dives/Thermal Protection System|Thermal Protection System]] — Starship survives reentry by covering its hottest surfaces with individually attached ceramic tiles that block heat from reaching the steel hull.

## Book V: Business And Mars

End with market disruption, funding logic, and the long-range settlement architecture.

- [[SpaceX/Business and Economics/Business and Economics Overview|Business and Economics Overview]] — SpaceX has disrupted the global launch industry by offering prices roughly 60–80% below legacy providers while maintaining profitability through reusability and vertical integration.
- [[SpaceX/Business and Economics/Commercial Launch Market|Commercial Launch Market]] — SpaceX turned launch from a scarce, premium service into a more price-competitive transportation market.
- [[SpaceX/Business and Economics/Competition Landscape|Competition Landscape]] — SpaceX changed the rules of competition in launch by making low cost and reusability the baseline rather than the exception.
- [[SpaceX/Business and Economics/Cost Revolution in Spaceflight|Cost Revolution in Spaceflight]] — Reusable rockets and high launch cadence dramatically lowered the cost per kilogram to orbit. Analogy: Like the transition from mainframes to PCs — what cost millions now costs thousands, unlocking new use cases.
- [[SpaceX/Business and Economics/Defense and National Security Contracts|Defense and National Security Contracts]] — SpaceX moved from outsider to core defense supplier by proving it could meet the military's highest launch and security standards.
- [[SpaceX/Business and Economics/NASA Contracts and Partnerships|NASA Contracts and Partnerships]] — NASA acted as the anchor customer that helped finance and validate SpaceX's early capabilities. Analogy: Like a startup's first enterprise customer — NASA's anchor contracts de-risked SpaceX's entire business.
- [[SpaceX/Business and Economics/SpaceX Funding and Valuation|SpaceX Funding and Valuation]] — SpaceX's valuation rose because investors increasingly believed its rockets and Starlink network could generate durable, large-scale cash flows.
- [[SpaceX/Mars and Beyond/Mars and Beyond Overview|Mars and Beyond Overview]] — Making life multiplanetary is the founding purpose of SpaceX — everything from Falcon 9's revenue to Starlink's cash flow to Starship's design is oriented toward establishing a self-sustaining human presence on Mars.
- [[SpaceX/Mars and Beyond/Beyond Mars|Beyond Mars]] — Starship is not just a Mars ship; it is a general-purpose deep-space transport platform whose capabilities spill over into many other missions.
- [[SpaceX/Mars and Beyond/In-Situ Resource Utilization|In-Situ Resource Utilization]] — ISRU uses local Martian resources to manufacture the propellant and consumables needed for sustained Mars operations.
- [[SpaceX/Mars and Beyond/Life Support for Mars|Life Support for Mars]] — A Mars mission needs life support that continuously recycles air and water, protects the crew, and keeps them functioning for years with minimal outside help.
- [[SpaceX/Mars and Beyond/Mars Base Design Concepts|Mars Base Design Concepts]] — Early Mars bases use what arrives first as shelter, then gradually add local construction, power, and shielding until the outpost becomes a real settlement.
- [[SpaceX/Mars and Beyond/Mars Colonization Architecture|Mars Colonization Architecture]] — Mars colonization works only if sending cargo and people becomes a repeated, scalable transportation cycle rather than a one-off expedition.
- [[SpaceX/Mars and Beyond/Mars Transit and Entry|Mars Transit and Entry]] — Getting to Mars is an orbital-timing problem, but landing there safely is an extreme guidance, thermal, and propulsion problem.
