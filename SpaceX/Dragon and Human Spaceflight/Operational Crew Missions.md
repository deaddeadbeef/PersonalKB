# Operational Crew Missions

> **Operational Crew Dragon flights created a regular, reusable, multinational crew-rotation system for the ISS beginning with Crew-1 in November 2020.**

## 🎯 Intuition
**The Core Idea:** Once Crew Dragon entered service, NASA and its partners gained a repeatable launch-to-station-to-return cycle for rotating long-duration ISS crews.

**Analogy:** It works like a scheduled orbital airline route: launch from Florida, reach the station, stay for months, then come back for recovery and turnaround.

**Why It Matters:** These missions ended the United States' nine-year dependence on Soyuz for routine ISS crew access. They also proved that a human-rated spacecraft capsule could be **reused** for later crewed flights, increasing cadence and lowering cost. Operational Crew Dragon has become the backbone of ISS crew transportation for NASA and international partners.

## ⚙️ Core Mechanics
### Key Specifications
- **First operational mission**: **Crew-1**, launched **November 2020**.
- **Standard crew size**: **4 astronauts** per mission.
- **Launch site**: **Kennedy Space Center, LC-39A**.
- **Docking timeline**: typically **24–30 hours** after launch.
- **Typical station stay**: about **150–200 days**, with some missions extending beyond **200 days**.
- **Recovery**: ocean splashdown with SpaceX recovery fleet, usually retrieved within about **1 hour**.
- **Capsule reuse**: began with **Crew-3**, using capsule **C210**, previously flown on **Crew-1**.
- **International participation**: astronauts from **NASA, ESA, JAXA, CSA, and Roscosmos** have flown.

### Key Facts
Each operational Crew Dragon mission follows a standard profile: launch aboard **Falcon 9** from **LC-39A** at Kennedy Space Center, enter **low-Earth orbit**, autonomously dock to an **IDA port** on the ISS **Harmony** module within roughly **24–30 hours**, remain docked for a long-duration station stay of around **150–200 days**, then undock, perform a deorbit burn, survive reentry under the **PICA-X** heat shield, and descend under parachutes to an ocean splashdown in the **Atlantic Ocean** or **Gulf of Mexico**. SpaceX recovery vessels then retrieve both capsule and crew shortly after landing.

The operational model has increasingly emphasized **reusability**. SpaceX began reflights of human-rated Crew Dragon capsules with **Crew-3**, when capsule **C210** flew again after its earlier **Crew-1** mission. The spacecraft are inspected, refurbished, and recertified between flights, and several capsules have now completed **third or later flights**. This has helped normalize higher launch cadence and lower recurring cost.

Operational missions have also grown more international. Crews have included astronauts from **ESA, JAXA, CSA**, and **Roscosmos**, including **Anna Kikina on Crew-5** under a seat-exchange arrangement. The note also preserves the current competitive context: **Boeing's Starliner Crew Flight Test launched in June 2024**, but the program's operational timeline remains significantly behind Crew Dragon.

## 🔬 Deep Dive
### Design / Engineering Details
Operational Crew Dragon missions matter not just because they fly, but because they repeat. A regular cadence means spacecraft processing, astronaut training, ISS planning, and international seat assignments can all be scheduled around a predictable transport system. Reuse is central to that model: the capsules are not disposable one-off vehicles, but fleet assets that cycle through inspection, refurbishment, recertification, and reflights. This is a major break from earlier human-spaceflight practice.
