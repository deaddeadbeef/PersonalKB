## Summary

SpaceX's point-to-point (P2P) Earth transport concept proposes using Starship for suborbital intercity passenger travel, with headline claims of New York to Tokyo in approximately 30 minutes and London to Sydney in under an hour. First presented at IAC 2017 alongside the BFR architecture reveal, the concept envisions Starship launching from offshore platforms, flying a suborbital ballistic trajectory reaching space, and landing on a similar platform near the destination city. The theoretical appeal is straightforward: suborbital ballistic trajectories are dramatically faster than subsonic air travel for intercontinental distances. However, the practical challenges are formidable and largely unresolved. Noise is perhaps the most immediate barrier — a Starship launch produces acoustic energy measured in the hundreds of decibels at close range, far exceeding any commercial airport noise standard. Offshore launch and landing platforms mitigate but do not eliminate this problem, and they introduce their own logistical challenges: passenger transfer from shore to platform, weather sensitivity for maritime operations, and the capital cost of building floating spaceports. Safety certification presents a regulatory challenge without precedent. Commercial aviation achieves roughly one fatal accident per ten million flights. Rocket launch reliability, even for the most mature vehicles, is orders of magnitude worse. The FAA has no framework for certifying a rocket as a commercial passenger transport vehicle, and developing one would require a safety record that cannot be established without extensive operational flight history — a chicken-and-egg problem. Regulatory hurdles extend beyond the FAA to international airspace coordination, overflight rights (suborbital trajectories cross sovereign airspace of multiple nations), and landing-site country regulations. Economics present another challenge: Musk suggested ticket prices comparable to full-fare economy airline tickets, but analysis suggests per-seat costs would need to account for vehicle amortization, propellant, offshore platform operations, and the low flight rate inherent in booster turnaround times. Military logistics applications have received more serious consideration. The US Transportation Command (USTRANSCOM) has expressed interest in using rocket-based transport for rapid global cargo delivery — moving 80 tons of material anywhere on Earth in under an hour. Military applications face lower safety-certification barriers (accepting higher risk), carry no passenger-noise concerns for cargo, and address a genuine capability gap in strategic airlift response time.

## Key Claims

- Suborbital Starship transport could theoretically connect New York to Tokyo in approximately 30 minutes via ballistic trajectory through space.
- Launch noise far exceeding commercial airport standards necessitates offshore platforms, which introduce significant logistical and capital-cost challenges.
- No FAA or international regulatory framework exists for certifying a rocket as a commercial passenger transport vehicle, creating a fundamental certification obstacle.
- Military cargo transport via Starship addresses a genuine capability gap and faces lower regulatory barriers than commercial passenger service.
- Per-seat economics remain unproven, with analysis suggesting costs substantially higher than Musk's suggested airline-comparable ticket prices.

## Significance

P2P Earth transport is the most speculative application in SpaceX's portfolio and may never materialize for commercial passengers. However, the military logistics variant addresses a real operational need and could provide development funding and flight-rate experience that eventually improves the commercial case — a pattern consistent with SpaceX's strategy of finding near-term revenue to fund long-term ambitions.
