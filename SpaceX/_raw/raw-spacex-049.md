## Summary

In late 2018, Elon Musk announced that SpaceX would abandon the carbon fiber composite design originally planned for Starship (then called BFR) in favor of 304L stainless steel—a decision that surprised the aerospace industry and became one of the most debated material choices in modern rocket engineering. The rationale centered on a counterintuitive property of austenitic stainless steels: their yield and ultimate tensile strength actually increase at cryogenic temperatures, the opposite behavior of aluminum alloys and carbon fiber composites. Combined with dramatically lower raw material costs, easier manufacturing, higher temperature tolerance, and faster design iteration, stainless steel offered a compelling systems-level advantage despite its higher density.

## Key Claims

- SpaceX originally designed Starship (BFR) using carbon fiber composite construction, with prototype tooling already in development, before Musk directed the switch to 304L stainless steel in approximately November–December 2018.
- 304L stainless steel exhibits increasing strength at cryogenic temperatures (yield strength approximately doubles from room temperature to liquid nitrogen temperatures), meaning the propellant tanks are actually strongest when fully loaded—a significant structural efficiency advantage.
- Raw material cost for 304L stainless steel is approximately $2.50 per kilogram, compared to $130 or more per kilogram for aerospace-grade carbon fiber prepreg, representing roughly a 50x cost reduction in material alone.
- Stainless steel is vastly easier to weld, bend, cut, and repair with standard industrial equipment compared to carbon fiber, which requires autoclaves, specialized layup tooling, and non-destructive inspection of every ply.
- The weight penalty of steel (density ~8,000 kg/m³ vs. ~1,600 kg/m³ for carbon fiber) is partially offset by cryogenic strength gains (allowing thinner walls), higher temperature tolerance (reducing TPS requirements), and the elimination of an internal metal liner that composite tanks require.

## Atomic Facts

1. The 304L designation indicates a low-carbon variant of 304 stainless steel, which improves weldability and resistance to intergranular corrosion—critical properties for a welded cryogenic pressure vessel that undergoes repeated thermal cycling.
2. Carbon fiber composites can experience microcracking at cryogenic temperatures due to differential thermal expansion between the fiber and matrix, potentially creating leak paths in propellant tank walls—a risk that stainless steel does not share.
3. Stainless steel's higher melting point (approximately 1,400–1,450°C) compared to aluminum alloys (~660°C) means the Starship airframe can tolerate significantly more heat soak-through during reentry before structural compromise, reducing the thermal protection system mass requirements.
4. The switch to stainless steel enabled SpaceX to begin building full-scale Starship prototypes within months at Boca Chica using relatively simple fabrication facilities, rather than the multi-year timeline required to build carbon fiber layup tooling and autoclaves.
5. SpaceX's iterative prototyping approach—building, testing to failure, and rapidly rebuilding—is economically feasible with $2.50/kg steel in a way that would be prohibitively expensive with $130+/kg carbon fiber.
6. Musk has publicly noted that stainless steel's reflectivity also provides a modest thermal advantage, reflecting a portion of reentry radiative heating that a dark carbon fiber surface would absorb.

## Significance

The stainless steel decision exemplifies SpaceX's willingness to challenge aerospace orthodoxy when systems-level analysis reveals a better path. While individual metrics favor carbon fiber (lower density) or aluminum (established aerospace heritage), the holistic evaluation—cost, cryogenic performance, manufacturability, repairability, temperature tolerance, and iteration speed—favored stainless steel for Starship's specific mission profile and production philosophy.
