# Starlink Satellite Generations

## Summary

Starlink satellites have evolved through five hardware generations since 2019. The v0.9 batch comprised 60 demonstration satellites launched May 2019, validating flat-pack deployment and basic broadband relay. The v1.0 generation (~260 kg) entered production in November 2019, featuring flat-panel stacking (60 per Falcon 9), krypton ion thrusters, and Ku/Ka-band phased arrays—but lacked inter-satellite links. The v1.5, introduced September 2021, added laser inter-satellite links (~100 Gbps), enabling satellite-to-satellite routing essential for oceanic and polar coverage. The v2 Mini (~800 kg), first launched February 2023, offers roughly 4x throughput per satellite versus v1.5, includes direct-to-cell hardware, and fits in the Falcon 9 fairing at 21–23 per launch. The full-size v2 (~1,250 kg) requires Starship for deployment. SpaceX iterated on brightness mitigation across generations—VisorSat sun shades on v1.0, dielectric mirror coatings on later versions—to address astronomer concerns.

## Key Claims

- Each satellite generation has roughly doubled or more the per-satellite throughput capacity, with v2 Mini estimated at approximately 4x the capacity of v1.5
- Laser inter-satellite links introduced with v1.5 fundamentally changed the network architecture by removing mandatory ground station relay hops
- The v2 Mini design represents the maximum satellite capability achievable within the Falcon 9 fairing volume and mass constraints
- Full-size v2 satellites are designed around Starship's capabilities, creating a direct dependency between the constellation's future capacity and Starship operational status
- SpaceX iterated on brightness mitigation across every generation in response to pressure from the astronomical community

## Atomic Facts

1. The v0.9 batch of 60 prototypes launched on May 23, 2019, creating the iconic "Starlink train" visible to the naked eye that sparked widespread public and astronomer attention
2. v1.0 satellites used krypton rather than xenon for their Hall-effect ion thrusters, reducing propellant costs by roughly an order of magnitude at the expense of some specific impulse
3. Laser inter-satellite links on v1.5 and later use optical wavelengths and achieve approximately 100 Gbps throughput with latency advantages over terrestrial fiber for long-distance routing
4. v2 Mini satellites weigh approximately 800 kg each, limiting Falcon 9 to 21–23 per launch compared to 60 for v1.0, which significantly impacts deployment cadence
5. The full-size v2 satellite at ~1,250 kg is too large for Falcon 9 and requires Starship, which has not yet achieved operational cargo deployment as of early 2024
6. VisorSat sun shades were added to v1.0 starting in mid-2020, reducing satellite brightness by roughly 1–1.5 magnitudes; later generations use integrated dielectric mirror films instead

## Significance

The rapid generational progression of Starlink hardware demonstrates SpaceX's vertically integrated manufacturing advantage and iterative engineering philosophy. Each generation addresses specific operational limitations—inter-satellite links remove ground station dependency, larger antennas increase capacity, and direct-to-cell opens entirely new markets. The v2/Starship dependency creates a critical strategic linkage between SpaceX's two major programs, where Starship delays directly constrain Starlink's future capacity growth.
