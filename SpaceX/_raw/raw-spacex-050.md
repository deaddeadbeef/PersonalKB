## Summary

Hot staging—the technique of igniting an upper stage's engines before physical separation from the booster—was introduced to the Starship program beginning with Integrated Flight Test 2 (IFT-2) on November 18, 2023. Unlike traditional staging where the first stage shuts down, the stages physically separate, and then the upper stage ignites (a sequence called "cold staging"), hot staging overlaps engine ignition with the separation event. This eliminates the coast phase between stages, maintaining continuous thrust and improving payload performance by approximately 10%. While the concept has heritage in Titan rockets and Soviet launch vehicles like the R-7 and Soyuz families, implementing it at Starship's unprecedented scale required novel engineering solutions including a dedicated hot-stage ring to vent exhaust gases.

## Key Claims

- Traditional cold staging involves a sequential process—booster engine shutdown, physical separation via mechanical systems, a brief coast phase, then upper stage engine ignition—during which gravity losses reduce payload performance.
- Hot staging ignites the Ship's Raptor vacuum and sea-level engines while still mechanically connected to the Super Heavy booster, with separation occurring milliseconds to seconds after upper stage engine confirmation, eliminating the coast-phase gravity loss entirely.
- SpaceX developed a dedicated hot-stage ring—a vented interstage structure positioned between the booster and Ship—that redirects the Ship's engine exhaust outward and away from the booster's forward dome during the overlap period.
- IFT-2 was the first Starship flight to employ hot staging, and the technique successfully achieved stage separation, though the booster was subsequently lost during its boostback burn due to an unrelated engine-out and resulting RUD.
- The performance benefit of hot staging is estimated at approximately 10% increased payload to orbit, a significant gain achieved without hardware mass increases or engine performance improvements.

## Atomic Facts

1. The hot-stage ring is a cylindrical steel structure with large venting ports that sits atop the Super Heavy booster, allowing the Ship's engine exhaust to escape laterally rather than impinging directly on the booster's forward tank dome.
2. During hot staging, the Ship's engines ignite and ramp to thrust while separation bolts or a mechanical release system holds the stack together; once engine performance is confirmed, the release is commanded and the stages separate.
3. Titan II, III, and IV rockets used hot staging operationally for decades, with the second stage igniting through a hole in the interstage while the first stage was still thrusting—providing direct engineering heritage for the concept.
4. Soviet and Russian rockets, including the Soyuz family derived from the R-7, employ a form of hot staging where strap-on boosters separate while the core stage is already firing, demonstrating the technique across thousands of flights.
5. The thermal and structural loads on the hot-stage ring are extreme: it must withstand direct exposure to multiple Raptor engine exhaust plumes (temperatures exceeding 3,000°C in the core flow) for several seconds while maintaining structural integrity for clean separation.
6. After IFT-2 demonstrated successful hot staging, subsequent flights (IFT-3 through IFT-5) continued to use the technique, with the hot-stage ring eventually designed to be jettisoned after separation to reduce booster return mass.

## Significance

Hot staging represents a meaningful performance upgrade achieved through operational technique rather than propulsion technology advancement. The approximately 10% payload gain compounds the already substantial capacity of Starship, and the successful demonstration across multiple IFTs has validated the approach for operational use. The technique's implementation at Starship scale—with 33 booster engines and 6 Ship engines involved—marks the largest hot-staging event ever attempted.
