## Summary

The transit and entry phases of a Starship Mars mission represent some of the most demanding engineering challenges in the entire colonization architecture. The baseline trajectory follows a near-Hohmann transfer orbit, requiring approximately 6 to 9 months of cruise depending on the specific launch window geometry and available propellant margin. These windows open roughly every 26 months when Earth and Mars reach favorable orbital alignment, with optimal windows offering lower delta-v requirements and shorter transit times. Faster trajectories are possible but require significantly more propellant, directly reducing payload mass to the surface — a trade that may favor cargo missions optimizing for mass over crew missions optimizing for reduced radiation exposure and consumables. The delta-v budget for Trans-Mars Injection (TMI) is approximately 3.6 km/s from low Earth orbit. Mars Orbit Insertion (MOI) in a traditional sense is avoided entirely; instead, Starship performs direct atmospheric entry, converting orbital velocity into heat through aerodynamic braking. The MOI-equivalent delta-v of approximately 0.6 km/s is shed through aerobraking rather than propulsive burn, saving hundreds of tons of propellant. Mars entry velocity is approximately 5.5 km/s, substantially lower than Earth reentry speeds but complicated by Mars's thin CO2 atmosphere — surface pressure is roughly 0.6% of Earth's. The thin atmosphere provides less deceleration per unit altitude, extending the heating period and requiring a heat shield optimized for different thermal loads than Earth reentry. Starship's entry profile leverages its bellyflop orientation, presenting maximum cross-section to the airflow for peak drag. After decelerating through the hypersonic and supersonic regimes, the vehicle executes the signature flip maneuver — rotating from horizontal belly-down attitude to vertical orientation for propulsive final landing. This maneuver, proven in Earth-based Starship tests, must function reliably in Mars's lower gravity (0.38g) and thinner atmosphere, where aerodynamic control surfaces have reduced authority.

## Key Claims

- Hohmann-type transfers to Mars require approximately 6 to 9 months of transit time, with trajectory duration varying by launch window geometry.
- The TMI delta-v budget from low Earth orbit is approximately 3.6 km/s, with faster trajectories requiring proportionally more propellant at the cost of payload mass.
- Direct atmospheric entry at Mars replaces traditional orbit insertion, saving the roughly 0.6 km/s MOI delta-v equivalent in propellant by converting velocity to heat.
- Mars atmospheric entry occurs at approximately 5.5 km/s, with the 0.6% surface pressure CO2 atmosphere providing less braking force and extending thermal loading duration.
- The bellyflop-to-vertical flip maneuver developed for Earth landings must be re-validated for Mars conditions including 0.38g gravity and reduced aerodynamic surface authority.

## Atomic Facts

1. A near-Hohmann transfer from Earth to Mars takes approximately 6 to 9 months depending on the specific synodic window.
2. Trans-Mars Injection from LEO requires approximately 3.6 km/s of delta-v.
3. Starship bypasses Mars orbit insertion by performing direct entry, descent, and landing (EDL) from the interplanetary trajectory.
4. Mars entry velocity is approximately 5.5 km/s.
5. Mars surface atmospheric pressure is roughly 0.6% of Earth's sea-level pressure, with an atmosphere composed predominantly of CO2.
6. Mars surface gravity is approximately 0.38g (3.72 m/s²), affecting both aerodynamic maneuvering and propulsive landing requirements.

## Significance

Mars EDL is often called the "seven minutes of terror" for robotic landers — for a vehicle the mass of Starship, it becomes an unprecedented engineering challenge. No vehicle of this size has ever attempted Mars entry, and the thin atmosphere creates a narrow corridor between insufficient deceleration and excessive thermal loading.
