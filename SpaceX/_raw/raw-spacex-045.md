## Summary

Starship's thermal protection system (TPS) represents a fundamental redesign of reentry heat shielding philosophy compared to the Space Shuttle and other heritage vehicles. The windward (belly) side of the Starship upper stage is covered with approximately 18,000 hexagonal ceramic tiles made from a silica-based material, mechanically attached to the stainless steel airframe rather than bonded with adhesive. This design decision prioritizes rapid individual tile replacement and reuse without refurbishment—key requirements for SpaceX's vision of aircraft-like turnaround times. Flight data from the Integrated Flight Test campaign has provided critical real-world validation of TPS performance under actual reentry heating conditions.

## Key Claims

- Approximately 18,000 hexagonal heat shield tiles cover the windward surface of Starship, each mechanically fastened to studs welded to the stainless steel substrate, allowing individual tile removal and replacement without disturbing adjacent tiles.
- The tiles are composed of a silica-based ceramic material that provides thermal insulation while remaining lightweight; the hexagonal geometry minimizes thermal expansion gaps and distributes aerodynamic loads more evenly than rectangular tiles.
- Unlike the Space Shuttle's TPS, which used RTV adhesive bonding (a labor-intensive process requiring days of cure time per tile), Starship's mechanical attachment system is designed for rapid field replacement, targeting maintenance timelines measured in hours rather than weeks.
- IFT reentry data revealed localized heating anomalies at tile gaps and flap hinge lines, areas where aerodynamic flow channeling concentrates thermal energy; SpaceX has iteratively improved gap filler designs between flights.
- SpaceX has explored transpiration cooling as a future TPS augmentation, where a small amount of fluid is forced through a porous tile surface to create an evaporative cooling boundary layer, potentially enabling even higher reentry velocities.

## Atomic Facts

1. Each hexagonal tile is approximately 15–20 centimeters across and designed as a standalone thermal protection unit, meaning a single damaged tile can be swapped without removing or disturbing neighboring tiles.
2. The mechanical attachment system uses metallic studs resistance-welded to the stainless steel skin, with tiles secured via clips or pins—a design that accommodates differential thermal expansion between the ceramic tile and steel substrate.
3. Stainless steel's higher temperature tolerance (compared to aluminum) means the TPS can allow some heat soak-through without structural compromise, reducing the required tile thickness and total TPS mass.
4. Flap hinge lines represent a particularly challenging TPS region because the gap geometry changes dynamically during reentry as control surfaces actuate, creating variable-width gaps exposed to hypersonic flow.
5. Gap fillers between tiles prevent hot gas ingestion into the sub-tile cavity; IFT data showed gap filler erosion as a primary failure mode, leading to localized overheating of the steel substrate.
6. Transpiration cooling remains a research concept rather than a near-term implementation, requiring porous tile materials and a fluid supply system that adds complexity but could dramatically extend TPS thermal margins.

## Significance

The TPS is a make-or-break system for Starship reusability. A heat shield that can survive repeated reentries without extensive refurbishment is essential to achieving the flight rates and cost targets that justify the entire Starship architecture. The iterative improvements observed across IFT flights demonstrate that SpaceX is converging on a viable solution, though gap management and flap protection remain active engineering challenges.
