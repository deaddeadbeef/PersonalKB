# Responsibility to Defend Earth 2024

## What This Source Is

Policy and ethics discussion (2024) on global coordination for existential threats — asteroid deflection, climate tipping points, pandemic response, and by extension any scenario requiring species-level decision-making. Draws on international law, the Responsibility to Protect (R2P) doctrine, and existential risk scholarship.

## Why It Matters to PHM

Eva Stratt's authority in the novel — unilateral power to commandeer resources, override national sovereignty, and coerce individuals for the mission — is the central ethical tension of the story. This source examines whether such authority could ever be legitimate, what governance structures might support it, and why current institutions are poorly equipped for existential-scale coordination.

## Key Takeaways

- Existential threats (those risking human extinction or civilizational collapse) arguably justify extraordinary measures, but the threshold for "existential" is contested.
- Current international governance (UN, treaties) lacks the authority, speed, or enforcement capability to coordinate a species-level emergency response.
- The Responsibility to Protect (R2P) doctrine provides a partial precedent: sovereignty can be overridden when a state fails to protect its population. But R2P has never been applied to global existential threats.
- Legitimate emergency authority requires accountability, proportionality, and sunset clauses — exactly what Stratt lacks in the novel.
- The gap between what existential threats demand and what democratic governance can deliver is a genuine unsolved problem in political philosophy.
