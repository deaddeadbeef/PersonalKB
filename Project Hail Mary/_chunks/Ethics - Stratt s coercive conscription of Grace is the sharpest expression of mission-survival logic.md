# Stratt's coercive conscription of Grace is the sharpest expression of mission-survival logic

## Context

In Chapter 22's flashback, after the Baikonur accident kills the primary science officer, Grace is the only remaining candidate who is both scientifically qualified and physiologically coma-resistant. Stratt informs him he has been selected; Grace refuses, insisting he does not want to die. Stratt calls him a coward. When Grace delivers his final refusal, a Russian soldier blocks his exit. Stratt reveals her plan: Grace will be held in a cell until launch, sedated, and loaded aboard unconscious. A French DGSE retrograde-amnesia drug will erase his recent memory, preserving his trained skills while preventing sabotage. Grace is given no choice.

## Why It Matters

This is the novel's ethical fulcrum. Every other coercive act by Stratt (pardons, nuclear detonations, IP seizures) affects populations or institutions. This one targets a single person — the protagonist — and the reader experiences it directly. The scene forces the question the novel has been circling: is any individual's autonomy inviolable when the alternative is species extinction? Stratt's answer is no, and the novel does not clearly refute her. Grace's amnesia in Chapter 1 is now fully explained, and his character arc is reframed: he did not volunteer.
