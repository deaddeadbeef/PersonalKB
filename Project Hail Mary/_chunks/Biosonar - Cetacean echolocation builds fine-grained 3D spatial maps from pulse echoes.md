# Cetacean echolocation builds fine-grained 3D spatial maps from pulse echoes

## Context

Odontocete biosonar — the echolocation system of dolphins, porpoises, and toothed whales — generates high-frequency directional clicks (10–150 kHz in bottlenose dolphins) focused into a forward acoustic beam by the melon, a fatty lens structure in the forehead. Returning echoes are received via the lower jaw and fat bodies, and processed by dedicated neural circuits in the brainstem and auditory cortex. The information content of a single echo is substantial: delay encodes range, inter-receiver timing differences and beam shape encode bearing, echo amplitude and spectral shape encode size, surface texture, and material properties. Bottlenose dolphins can discriminate cylindrical targets differing by less than 0.5 mm in wall thickness at ranges of several meters. This is not general "hearing" — it is active, directed, information-dense environmental scanning.

## Why It Matters

If Eridians evolved acoustic perception as their primary sense in a high-pressure, optically opaque atmosphere, the question is: can a sonar-first sensory system support intelligence, engineering, and complex communication? Real cetacean biosonar answers yes — at least for fine-grained spatial perception. An Eridian acoustic-primary sensory system could, in principle, support 3D environmental modeling, object recognition, and even rudimentary material-property assessment (distinguishing metals from ceramics by echo timbre). The novel's treatment of Rocky as an engineer who perceives shape and material through sound is not biologically absurd.
