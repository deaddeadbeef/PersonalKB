# UHTC oxide layers act as diffusion barriers that slow further degradation

## Context

When a UHTC like hafnium diboride (HfB₂) or zirconium diboride (ZrB₂) is exposed to oxidizing environments at moderate temperatures, its surface reacts to form a thin oxide layer (HfO₂ or ZrO₂). This oxide layer is dense and low-permeability, acting as a barrier that slows further oxygen diffusion into the ceramic bulk. The mechanism is analogous to the alumina scale that protects nickel superalloys in turbine blades. Above roughly 1600–1800°C, however, boron trioxide (B₂O₃) and other volatile oxides evaporate faster than the protective scale can form. Once scale volatilization dominates, the underlying ceramic is exposed directly and degrades rapidly.

## Why It Matters

Xenonite in *Project Hail Mary* must maintain permeability barriers between incompatible atmospheres — human air and Eridian high-pressure ammonia — across tunnel walls and pressure hull boundaries. The real physics of diffusion barriers in ceramics is directly relevant: the question of whether a material can maintain a controlled permeability boundary under extreme conditions is exactly the question the novel asks of xenonite. The Taumoeba-82.5 permeability crisis (where a modified Taumoeba strain causes xenonite permeability to break down) parallels the real failure mode of a ceramic whose diffusion-barrier properties are selectively compromised.
