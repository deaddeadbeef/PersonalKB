# Stratt's "war, famine, pestilence, and death" framing articulates the existential stakes

## Context

In Chapter 25's flashback, hours before launch, Stratt visits Grace in his Baikonur cell. She does not apologise. Instead, she delivers a compressed history lecture: for fifty thousand years, human civilisation was defined entirely by food acquisition; the industrial revolution gave modern people a false sense of security; Astrophage will strip that away. The projected consequences — compounded by war over scarce food and medical infrastructure collapse — are apocalyptic. "War, famine, pestilence, and death." She says she had no ethical alternative. Grace tells her to go to hell. She agrees, noting hell is already coming for everyone on Earth.

## Why It Matters

This is Stratt's moral self-accounting and the novel's most direct engagement with the ethical logic of existential response. Unlike her earlier decisions (which are presented through action), this scene gives Stratt a voice to articulate her reasoning explicitly. The Four Horsemen framing is deliberately blunt — Stratt does not soften her position or seek forgiveness. The exchange also completes the Grace-Stratt relationship: his last conscious memory before the amnesia drug is this confrontation, giving it structural weight as the final flashback-strand beat.
